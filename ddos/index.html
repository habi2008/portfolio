DDoS

Distributed Denial of Service

What it means

A DDoS attack stands for Distributed Denial of Service. It is a situation where a website or online service is flooded with more requests than it can handle, causing it to slow down or stop working for real users.

The key idea is that the system itself is not broken or hacked. Instead, it is overwhelmed. Because the requests come from many different places at once, the service struggles to stay available.

Think of it this way:
Imagine a store with one entrance. If thousands of people try to walk in at the same time, nobody gets inside — even though the store is still open and fully functional.

Why people study DDoS

DDoS attacks help beginners understand why websites go down even when no data is stolen. Availability is just as important as security, and overload alone can make a system unusable.

For developers and engineers, DDoS reveals how systems behave under extreme pressure and why handling traffic spikes is a core part of modern system design.

For the nerds 👀 (expand if curious)

From a deeper perspective, DDoS attacks exploit resource limits such as bandwidth, connection handling, request queues, or compute time. Even perfectly secure systems can fail when shared resources are exhausted faster than they can recover.

This is why real-world defenses focus on traffic distribution, rate limiting, redundancy, and absorbing attacks rather than trying to block every request. At scale, the challenge is not identifying attackers — it is surviving load.
